The data behind the Design Storm, explained

Five linked views of the Denver Water challenge data: how mountain snow becomes river flow, how storms move water quality downstream, and what arrives at the Foothills treatment plant. Companion to the 3D system map; every chart is drawn from the committed files in this repository, nothing is invented.
Provisional data: USGS, NRCS and NOAA revise readings after publication. Denver Water's data terms apply (TERMS.md).

The system as one timeline

Five stacked panels in the order the water moves: snowpack melts, rain falls, the river rises, its quality shifts, and days later the treatment plant feels it. Drag on the small strip to zoom a period; hover anywhere to read every panel at the same date.

Why this data

These are exactly the predictor chains Jake's soft-sensor models and the 24-hour proof of concept used: Hoosier Pass snow water equivalent (the station that replaced Michigan Creek), NOAA precipitation (the DWR gauge's own precip counter is a dirty cumulative and is excluded), DWR streamflow at Waterton, the USGS sentinel-gage quality readings above Strontia Springs, and Denver Water's own lab record at the Foothills influent — the two numbers everyone is trying to see coming. Seeing them stacked is the fastest way to understand why the models lean on turbidity × flow and conductance.

Data hygiene applied to these views

    Strontia Springs Reservoir, by depth

    The profiling sonde takes readings all the way down the water column — 16,093 of them across Apr 7 to Aug 19, 2026. Each pixel column is one day; depth runs downward, like the reservoir. Watch the warm surface layer form in June: that is stratification, and it decides which water the dam sends to the plant.

    Why this data

    This is the newest and least-explored dataset in the repository, and the heart of Scenario 2: unlike every daily series, it resolves depth. The 24-hour proof of concept found it redundant for next-day prediction, but it is the right foundation for the multi-day reservoir-mixing question — the lag between the river and the plant lives in this water column. Vertical position is depth in metres (verified: low values read warm surface water).

    Anatomy of a storm

    Pick an event. Rain falls (top), the river answers within hours to days, turbidity spikes as the runoff arrives at the sentinel gage, and the plant's lab numbers move in the days after. The offsets printed on each event are measured from these files — they are the intuition behind every lag in Jake's models.

    Why this data

    The proof of concept showed the rare, large day-to-day changes in TOC are storm-driven, so storms are where advance warning matters. The Aug 14–15, 2026 event uses the committed 15-minute USGS series behind the 3D map's radar replay; the other events are the biggest precipitation days in the NOAA record with their local flow and turbidity responses. One caution: the NOAA gauge is a single point in a 4,000-square-mile watershed — a storm can soak the upper basin and miss it entirely.

    The seasonal rhythm, year over year

    Every water year (Oct 1 to Sep 30) overlaid on one axis. The dashed line is the median of all years; pick a year to follow it through snowpack, runoff, and what the plant received. Wet 2023 and the leaner years read as different shapes, not just different sizes.

    Why this data

    The 3D map's influent panel says "the spring-runoff rhythm is the pattern to notice" — this view is that sentence as a chart, in the same fold-by-water-year idiom the map already uses for reservoirs and snow stations. It is Scenario 3's wet-against-dry comparison: snowpack sets the runoff, runoff sets the TOC pulse, and the plant's hard weeks arrive on the falling limb.

    How each sub-basin behaves

    Denver's water comes from five instrumented drainage basins on both sides of the Continental Divide — the dashed outlines on the 3D map. Each card shows a basin's snowpack against its own median, when the melt started and finished, and its outlet river. East-slope water flows to the plant by gravity; west-slope water only arrives through a tunnel.

    Why this data

    Basin polygons come from the USGS NLDI service (committed in strontia-brief/), member snow stations are assigned by point-in-polygon exactly as the 3D map does, and SWE history is the committed NRCS record behind the map's snow-station overlays. Outlet flows for the west-slope and nested gages need a one-time USGS fetch (fetch_basin_flows.py); the sentinel basin's flow is the committed DWR record. Sub-basin behaviour is the "where is the water coming from this year" context every prediction sits inside.

    Honest limitations

    These views build intuition; they do not claim predictive skill. The pre-registered 24-hour study in teams/poc-24h-toc-alk found that at 24 hours "tomorrow will look like today" is essentially unbeatable with this data (day-to-day R² ≈ 0.96 for both targets), and that upstream data's value plausibly lives at multi-day horizons, where the persistence forecast decays. The winter gap (no Jan–Mar lab values) is structural. The sonde covers one location and one partial season. Every reading here is provisional and subject to revision by USGS, NRCS, NOAA and DWR; Denver Water's data terms travel with every derived file in this folder.
